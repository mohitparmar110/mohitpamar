Ecommerce Conversion Funnel Analysis: Find Where Sales Are Lost

Do not start a conversion project by redesigning the page you dislike most. Start by finding where qualified shoppers actually stop moving forward.

Quick answer: Analyse the ecommerce journey as a sequence: landing or collection visit → product view → add to cart → checkout start → purchase. Find the largest meaningful drop, then segment it by device and traffic source. Only after that should you investigate the page, offer or technical issue responsible. A site-wide conversion rate can tell you that something changed; it rarely tells you what to fix.

Why overall conversion rate is not enough

Suppose sales fall this week. The immediate reaction might be to change the homepage, rewrite product copy or launch another discount. But the same final symptom can come from very different problems.

You may have attracted more low-intent traffic. Product pages may be getting views but not add-to-carts. Shoppers may be adding products successfully and then abandoning after seeing delivery terms. A payment issue may affect only one device. Each problem needs a different response.

The purpose of funnel analysis is simple: reduce the number of guesses.

Build a simple ecommerce funnel first

1. EntryDid the right visitor arrive?
2. ProductDid they reach a relevant PDP?
3. CartDid they show buying intent?
4. CheckoutDid they begin purchase?
5. PurchaseDid the order complete?

Your analytics setup may use more detailed events, but this basic structure is enough to start asking useful questions. Keep event definitions consistent. A broken or duplicated event can create a convincing-looking funnel that is still wrong.

Step 1: verify measurement before diagnosing behaviour

Test the journey yourself on mobile and desktop. Confirm that important events fire at the intended moment and that a single action is not being counted twice. Check whether payment redirects, consent settings or theme changes affect measurement.

  • Can you trace one test journey from entry to purchase?
  • Are product views recorded on the correct pages?
  • Does add-to-cart represent a successful cart action rather than a button click that may fail?
  • Is checkout start defined consistently?
  • Are completed purchases recorded once with sensible order values?

Do not make a large CRO decision from data you have not sanity-checked.

Step 2: find the stage where intent stops progressing

Traffic reaches the site but few people view products

Investigate landing-page relevance, navigation, collection merchandising and campaign intent. If an ad promises a specific product or offer but sends people to a generic destination, the problem may begin before the PDP. Use the ad-to-landing-page message match checklist to review that handoff.

Product views are healthy but add-to-cart is weak

This is a reason to inspect the product page, not proof that its design is bad. Check product clarity, media, price, variants, delivery information, proof and whether the product itself matches the traffic being sent to it. The Shopify product page CRO checklist gives a detailed review order.

Add-to-cart is healthy but checkout starts are weak

Look at the cart experience. Unexpected costs, unclear delivery thresholds, difficult quantity controls, coupon distractions or a cart drawer that behaves badly on mobile can interrupt strong purchase intent.

Checkout starts but purchases fall away

Investigate shipping, payment options, errors, address friction and any unexpected cost or policy revealed late. If checkout is platform-controlled, focus on the parts you can verify rather than assuming a visual redesign is the answer.

Step 3: segment before you average

An average can hide the useful pattern. At minimum, compare mobile and desktop and separate major traffic sources. If enough data exists, useful cuts can also include landing page, campaign, new versus returning visitors, product category and geography relevant to the business.

For example, a PDP may look healthy overall while paid mobile traffic has a much weaker add-to-cart path. That points toward a narrower investigation than redesigning the entire store.

Do not keep slicing until every segment is tiny. Small samples create noisy stories. Use segmentation to expose meaningful differences, not to manufacture certainty.

Step 4: connect the metric to the customer question

  • Low product discovery: “Am I in the right place, and can I find what I came for?”
  • Low add-to-cart: “Is this product right for me at this price?”
  • Low checkout start: “Do I still want this after seeing my cart and costs?”
  • Low purchase completion: “Can I complete this order comfortably and successfully?”

This framing keeps CRO connected to decisions rather than isolated interface elements.

Step 5: choose the next investigation, not the next redesign

Once you locate a weak stage, gather evidence around it. Review session behaviour where appropriate, test the flow yourself, inspect support questions, compare device layouts, check technical errors and read the actual page as a customer would.

Then write a specific problem statement. “The website needs better CRO” is not useful. “Paid mobile visitors reaching this PDP frequently view the product but do not add it to cart; the first screen hides the selected size state and delivery information is difficult to find” is something a team can investigate and test.

A practical funnel-analysis checklist

  1. Verify key analytics events.
  2. Define the main purchase stages.
  3. Compare progression between each stage.
  4. Check mobile versus desktop.
  5. Check major traffic sources and landing contexts.
  6. Identify the largest meaningful drop, not merely the ugliest page.
  7. Review the customer questions at that stage.
  8. Check technical and UX evidence.
  9. Form one specific hypothesis.
  10. Measure the change across the wider funnel, not only the local click.

Do not optimise a local metric at the expense of sales

A change can increase button clicks without improving purchases. An aggressive promotion can increase add-to-cart while reducing margin. Removing useful information may make a page shorter while sending more uncertainty into checkout or customer support.

Evaluate important changes against the business outcome and the next stages of the journey. If you are deciding whether to scale acquisition, the CRO before more ad spend framework provides the broader pre-scaling review.

FAQ

How do I find where my ecommerce conversion funnel is losing sales?

Break the journey into stages such as landing, product view, add to cart, checkout start and purchase. Compare the drop between stages, then segment by device and traffic source before deciding what needs investigation.

Is a low ecommerce conversion rate always a website problem?

No. Traffic quality, campaign promise, product fit, price, availability, page experience, shipping, payment and checkout can all affect conversion. The funnel helps narrow the cause.

Which funnel metric should I improve first?

Start with a meaningful drop that affects enough qualified users and can be investigated reliably. Avoid optimising a metric in isolation without checking the stages around it.
